How It Works

Five layers, one grid, one question each

Each layer optimises something different, which is why they stay separate. Occurrence and history target all fires; escape and assets target the ones that get away; arrival time and out-of-reach target what a crew can do about it.

  1. Occurrence

    Where a new fire is most likely tomorrow: the ignition forecast, distributed within each district by the official ignition record rather than the satellite.

  2. Escape

    Occurrence × the probability that a fire starting in that cell exceeds 10 ha, from vegetation, exposure and access: distance to roads, slope, travel time from settlements.

  3. Assets

    Escape × hectares of plantation within 3 km, weighted by stand structure. With your inventory (age, species, value, installations) this layer becomes your assets.

  4. Arrival time

    Minutes along the road network from the nearest base, accumulated along the shortest path on the grid. A 1 km approximation; it knows nothing about gates or track condition.

  5. Out of reach

    The escape risk that no base can reach within the chosen threshold of 20, 30 or 45 minutes. The sectors that stay far under any assumption are the ones worth a conversation.

Does It Work?

Twice the escaped fires in the same area

Verified against the official fire register, not satellite detections: 55,323 recorded fires in the pilot region, 2011–2025, of which 8% exceeded 10 ha. Every season held out of training.

The trade-off is stated: prioritising by escape catches fewer fires overall (14% against 31% in 1,000 km²), because it ignores the small ones that go out on their own. That is why Occurrence stays a separate layer.

What it is, and what it is not

  • Occurrence and escape, not spread. It does not simulate how a fire grows; your spread simulator does that once a fire exists. This layer decides where crews sleep tonight so that the fires most likely to escape are reached in time.
  • Hectares, not dollars. Assets are counted in plantation hectares, weighted by structure. Avoided losses in money need your inventory, your costs and an assessment of what would have happened without the crew, which is the end-of-season comparison a pilot sets up.
  • The bases are yours to supply. No public source lists an operator's crew bases, so the prototype places them by optimisation over historical ignitions in plantation areas. No coverage figure from assumed bases is presented as a result; what is shown is the relative effect of moving them and the sectors that stay far under any assumption.
  • Built on the official register. Escape is trained on fires recorded by the national fire agency, with the evaluated season held out, and verified against the same register the next day. With your own daily positioning record, April becomes a direct comparison against what you actually did.
