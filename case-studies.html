A Daily, Self-Verifying Ignition Forecast — 86 Districts of Southern Chile

The Problem

Protection managers in one of the most fire-exposed plantation regions in the world already have fire-weather alerts, spread simulators and a detection network. What they asked for, in their words, was where and when: which district, which day. Their positioning rule was historical frequency, and nothing on the market answered the question.

What We Built

In six weeks, from public data only: a panel of 85 variables per district-day across Ñuble, Biobío and La Araucanía (86 districts, 69,000 km², 2012–2025), a gradient-boosted occurrence model with isotonic calibration, a second model that distributes each district's probability to 1 km cells, separate models for two and three days out, and an unattended daily job on free cloud infrastructure that forecasts, publishes and verifies. A regional outlook card translates the day's index into expected fires, calibrated on eleven out-of-sample seasons. The 2022–23 season, including the February 2023 firestorm, can be replayed day by day with a model that never saw it.

The Outcome

Out of sample over eleven seasons, the ten districts prioritised each day capture 41% of next-day fires against 35% for historical frequency, winning every season; a fire-weather index alone captures 10%. At 1 km, 1,000 km² of zones capture 14.6% against 8.9%. Against the official fire register rather than satellite detections, 42% against 38%. The system has published every morning since 17 September 2026 and scores itself in public; that verified archive is the product, and it is the demo put in front of every customer.

Open data: NASA FIRMS, ERA5, OpenStreetMap, WorldPop, CONAF University of Canterbury

Product page: Wintel ignition forecast. The dashboard is private to pilot partners; walkthroughs on request.

From Where Fires Start to Where Crews Should Be Based — Southern Chile, 2022–23 Replayed

The Problem

An occurrence forecast catches the most fires, but most fires go out on their own. The ones that matter are the few that escape, and whether a crew can reach them in time. Operators position crews by experience and history; nobody told them which of tomorrow's fires would get away, what sat around them, or how far the nearest base was by road.

What We Built

A second dashboard on the same 1 km grid as the ignition forecast. An escape model trained on 55,323 fires from the national fire register (2011–2025, evaluated season held out) estimates per cell the probability a fire exceeds 10 ha from vegetation, exposure and accessibility; travel time from settlements turned out to be the strongest access signal, with a tenfold difference in escape rate between the nearest and farthest quintiles. Escape risk is multiplied by plantation hectares within 3 km, and arrival time is computed along the road network from each base by shortest path. Bases are placed by optimisation over historical ignitions, can be moved on the map with coverage recalculated in the browser, and exported as the file an operator's real bases replace. A verification tab scores every layer against the register's next-day fires.

The Outcome

Over eleven held-out seasons, weighting occurrence by escape doubles the escaped fires caught in the same 1,000 km²: 20% against 11%, winning every season, at the stated cost of catching fewer fires overall (14% against 31%). On the eve of the 2 February 2023 outbreak the regional escape index sat at the 98th percentile for the date. The prototype was shown to protection managers as work in progress, with the three asks that turn it into their tool: bases, inventory and a daily record of where crews were positioned.

Open data: CONAF fire register and land survey, OpenStreetMap, WorldPop, terrain tiles University of Canterbury

Product page: Wintel Risk & Deployment. Occurrence and escape, not spread; hectares, not dollars.

Multi-Region Evidence Manuscript submitted 2026

Next-Day Fire Occurrence Across Seven Regions on Four Continents

The Problem

Fire agencies decide every day where to pre-position crews, yet the operational guidance rests on fire-danger indices designed to describe fire behaviour, not to locate new fires. Whether a single engine built from public data could beat both that index and the historical-frequency heuristic, across very different fire regimes and with no local tuning, had not been tested.

What We Built

The same gradient-boosted occurrence model, built only from reanalysis weather, fire history and recent fire activity, evaluated on agency fire registers in Chile, Portugal, California, New Zealand, Mexico, the US South-east and Canada: 409,411 fires, 68 held-out fire seasons, 4.9 million unit-days. Benchmarks: historical frequency, the best fitted Fire Weather Index model, a self-exciting point process, and a re-implementation of ECMWF's Probability of Fire.

The Outcome

Ranked by the Fire Weather Index, units capture fewer of tomorrow's fires than the historical heuristic, even with perfect weather: the limitation is the index, not the weather forecast. The model beats the heuristic in 66 of 68 seasons and the best FWI model in all 68, catching 1.7 to 3.9 times more next-day fires than the index in the top 10% of units, depending on the region. Which information carries the skill depends on the ignition regime: recent human activity in Chile, California and Mexico; weather in Portugal, New Zealand and the US South-east. The model transfers to unseen regions and keeps most of its skill five days ahead with real weather forecasts.

University of Canterbury

Ardid, A. et al. Next-day wildfire occurrence across seven fire regimes on four continents. Manuscript submitted, 2026.

From Forecast Skill to Economic Value — Sub-Hourly Fire Potential Across Australian Regions

The Problem

Fire agencies plan against fire danger issued on a daily cycle, which cannot resolve the intraday swings that turn a containable fire into an emergency. And where a sharper forecast does exist, the harder question usually goes unanswered: is it worth acting on?

What We Built

Machine-learning models that turn automatic weather station data into sub-hourly wildfire potential forecasts across multiple Australian regions, including transfer-learning methods to carry a model from data-rich regions to data-poor ones. Forecast skill was then evaluated in a cost–loss framework against Fire Behaviour Index practice to derive the potential economic value of acting on the forecast.

The Outcome

A published, end-to-end case that sub-hourly forecasting is not only more skilful but economically defensible as early warning — expressed as the range of warning thresholds and cost–loss ratios over which the forecast returns value to the organisation using it.

Covey Associates (QLD) University of Canterbury Scion

Ardid, A., Power, A., Valencia, A., Pearce, H.G., Gross, S., Dempsey, D. & Boer, M. (2026). From forecast skill to economic value: sub-hourly wildfire potential forecasting across Australian regions. International Journal of Wildland Fire 35, WF25221. doi:10.1071/WF25221

Scale-Dependent Controls on Wildfire Growth — New Zealand

The Problem

Whether a fire stays small or escalates is governed by the fire-environment triangle of weather, topography and fuels — but the relative weight of those three changes with fire size in ways that had not been quantified for a temperate, human-dominated fire regime like New Zealand's. Fire-danger rating and resource allocation depend on getting that weighting right.

What We Built

An analysis of 116,771 wildfire records from New Zealand's national Wildfire Occurrence Database (1931–2022), combining meteorological observations, New Zealand Fire Danger Rating System variables, topography and fuel-type indicators. Balanced Bagged Trees with feature-dropout importance model the probability that a fire exceeds four cumulative size thresholds: 0.1, 1, 10 and 100 ha.

The Outcome

Fuel type and topography dominate discrimination from 0.1 to 10 ha, indicating strong landscape constraints on early growth. At 100 ha, atmospheric forcing becomes comparable to fuels and topography, each contributing roughly a third of total importance. Topographic control shifts from slope at smaller sizes to elevation at the largest threshold, while relative humidity, head-fire intensity and sustained wind become increasingly informative. Operationally: early growth is landscape-limited, escape is weather-driven — and fires cross size-specific environmental filters in sequence.

Scion / NZ Institute for Bioeconomy Science University of Canterbury

Ardid, A., Kiely, L. & Gross, S. Scale-dependent controls on wildfire growth in New Zealand: from landscape constraints to atmospheric forcing. Manuscript under review.

Projecting Fire-Growth Probability Under Climate Change — New Zealand

The Problem

Wildfire risk is projected to rise globally, yet spatially resolved projections are missing for many regions, New Zealand among them. Fire-weather-index approaches capture only part of what controls fire growth, and coarse global models cannot support planning at the scale councils and asset owners work at.

What We Built

A delta-ALE (accumulated local effects) spatial transfer learning method that bridges point-based machine-learning fire models and gridded climate projections. Each 5 km grid cell is anchored to its own historical climate baseline on ALE curves extracted from a balanced bagged tree classifier trained on ~117,000 New Zealand fire records, giving cell-specific changes in fire-growth probability under CMIP6 scenarios.

The Outcome

The probability of a fire exceeding 10 ha given ignition increases nationally by of order 0.5–1.9 percentage points (annual mean) by 2080–2099 across SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5. Temperature dominates the projected change even though relative humidity is the strongest predictor in the underlying model; spring increases approach present-day summer levels, consistent with fire-season lengthening; and fires ≥100 ha show roughly 1.7× larger relative increases. Bootstrap resampling and a range-based applicability check bound how far the numbers can be pushed, so the spatial pattern, scenario ranking and driver attribution carry the interpretation rather than the absolute magnitudes. The framework transfers to any region with a trained fire model and gridded climate projections.

Scion / NZ Institute for Bioeconomy Science University of Canterbury

Ardid, A., Kiely, L. & Gross, S. Accumulated local effects as spatial transfer functions: projecting wildfire growth probability under climate change. Manuscript under review. · Explore the interactive projection viewer
