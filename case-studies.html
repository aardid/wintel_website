Forecasting & Value Published 2026

From Forecast Skill to Economic Value — Sub-Hourly Fire Potential Across Australian Regions

The Problem

Fire agencies plan against fire danger issued on a daily cycle, which cannot resolve the intraday swings that turn a containable fire into an emergency. And where a sharper forecast does exist, the harder question usually goes unanswered: is it worth acting on?

What We Built

Machine-learning models that turn automatic weather station data into sub-hourly wildfire potential forecasts across multiple Australian regions, including transfer-learning methods to carry a model from data-rich regions to data-poor ones. Forecast skill was then evaluated in a cost–loss framework against Fire Behaviour Index practice to derive the potential economic value of acting on the forecast.

The Outcome

A published, end-to-end case that sub-hourly forecasting is not only more skilful but economically defensible as early warning — expressed as the range of warning thresholds and cost–loss ratios over which the forecast returns value to the organisation using it.

Covey Associates (QLD) University of Canterbury Scion

Ardid, A., Power, A., Valencia, A., Pearce, H.G., Gross, S., Dempsey, D. & Boer, M. (2026). From forecast skill to economic value: sub-hourly wildfire potential forecasting across Australian regions. International Journal of Wildland Fire 35, WF25221. doi:10.1071/WF25221

Scale-Dependent Controls on Wildfire Growth — New Zealand

The Problem

Whether a fire stays small or escalates is governed by the fire-environment triangle of weather, topography and fuels — but the relative weight of those three changes with fire size in ways that had not been quantified for a temperate, human-dominated fire regime like New Zealand's. Fire-danger rating and resource allocation depend on getting that weighting right.

What We Built

An analysis of 116,771 wildfire records from New Zealand's national Wildfire Occurrence Database (1931–2022), combining meteorological observations, New Zealand Fire Danger Rating System variables, topography and fuel-type indicators. Balanced Bagged Trees with feature-dropout importance model the probability that a fire exceeds four cumulative size thresholds: 0.1, 1, 10 and 100 ha.

The Outcome

Fuel type and topography dominate discrimination from 0.1 to 10 ha, indicating strong landscape constraints on early growth. At 100 ha, atmospheric forcing becomes comparable to fuels and topography, each contributing roughly a third of total importance. Topographic control shifts from slope at smaller sizes to elevation at the largest threshold, while relative humidity, head-fire intensity and sustained wind become increasingly informative. Operationally: early growth is landscape-limited, escape is weather-driven — and fires cross size-specific environmental filters in sequence.

Scion / NZ Institute for Bioeconomy Science University of Canterbury

Ardid, A., Kiely, L. & Gross, S. Scale-dependent controls on wildfire growth in New Zealand: from landscape constraints to atmospheric forcing. Manuscript under review.

Projecting Fire-Growth Probability Under Climate Change — New Zealand

The Problem

Wildfire risk is projected to rise globally, yet spatially resolved projections are missing for many regions, New Zealand among them. Fire-weather-index approaches capture only part of what controls fire growth, and coarse global models cannot support planning at the scale councils and asset owners work at.

What We Built

A delta-ALE (accumulated local effects) spatial transfer learning method that bridges point-based machine-learning fire models and gridded climate projections. Each 5 km grid cell is anchored to its own historical climate baseline on ALE curves extracted from a balanced bagged tree classifier trained on ~117,000 New Zealand fire records, giving cell-specific changes in fire-growth probability under CMIP6 scenarios.

The Outcome

The probability of a fire exceeding 10 ha given ignition increases nationally by of order 0.5–1.9 percentage points (annual mean) by 2080–2099 across SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5. Temperature dominates the projected change even though relative humidity is the strongest predictor in the underlying model; spring increases approach present-day summer levels, consistent with fire-season lengthening; and fires ≥100 ha show roughly 1.7× larger relative increases. Bootstrap resampling and a range-based applicability check bound how far the numbers can be pushed, so the spatial pattern, scenario ranking and driver attribution carry the interpretation rather than the absolute magnitudes. The framework transfers to any region with a trained fire model and gridded climate projections.

Scion / NZ Institute for Bioeconomy Science University of Canterbury

Ardid, A., Kiely, L. & Gross, S. Accumulated local effects as spatial transfer functions: projecting wildfire growth probability under climate change. Manuscript under review. · Explore the interactive projection viewer
