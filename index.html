The Problem

Wildfire protection starts after the fire has started

Detecting a fire, simulating its spread and dispatching crews are well served. The decision made before ignition, where to place crews, patrols and aircraft tomorrow, still relies on two blunt tools.

Fire-weather alerts

Say when conditions are dangerous across a whole region, not where fires will start. On their own they trigger costly over-dispatch.

Historical frequency

“Where you burned before, you burn again.” It ignores tomorrow's weather and last week's activity, and it is what most operators use today.

Who lives with this

Fire-protection managers at forestry companies and fire agencies, who position crews every day of the season. In New Zealand alone: 4,000–5,000 vegetation fires a year, 97% started by people.

The missing layer

Nobody forecasts where and when the next fire will start. Detection vendors act after ignition; spread and fire-weather systems say when, not where. Wintel is the layer in front of both.

Behind the Forecast

The same science, applied to the rest of the fire decision

The two dashboards are the products. Around them sit four published capabilities we bring to pilots and research partnerships: how fires escalate, what a forecast is worth, and how fire growth changes with the climate.

Sub-Hourly Fire Potential

Machine learning on live weather-station feeds, updating fire potential between the daily fire-danger cycles that operations run on today.

Escalation Probability by Fire Size

The probability a fire exceeds 0.1, 1, 10 or 100 ha given ignition, and which fuel, terrain and weather conditions govern each threshold.

Climate-Change Projections

Spatial transfer learning that carries a trained fire model onto 5 km CMIP6 climate grids, mapping where fire-growth probability rises, by how much, and driven by what.

Forecast Value, Not Just Skill

Cost–loss analysis and potential economic value: at which warning thresholds a forecast actually pays for the organisation acting on it.
